## WHY THIS WEEK MATTERS

Last week you stopped undermining yourself with weak language. Now you build
the instrument that delivers your words: your voice and your body.

Here's a fact that should change how you think about communication forever:
**research consistently shows that 55% of how you're perceived comes from body
language, 38% from vocal tone, and only 7% from the actual words you say**
(Mehrabian, 1971). You can have the most brilliant insight in the room — but
if you deliver it with a shaky voice, shifting feet, and darting eyes, the room
hears "uncertain."

This week is about building the physical and vocal chassis of authority. When
you walk into a room, people should feel your presence before you open your
mouth. When you speak, the WAY you speak should make people lean in.

This isn't about being loud. It's about being deliberate, grounded, and
impossible to ignore.

## PART 1: THE VOICE OF AUTHORITY

Your voice is an instrument. Most people have never tuned it.

### 1.1 Vocal Register — Go Lower

**The science:** Studies from McMaster University found that candidates with
lower-pitched voices are perceived as stronger, more competent, and more
trustworthy leaders. Both men and women benefit from a slightly lower register
— not unnaturally deep, but consciously anchored.

**What's happening now:** When you're nervous or excited, your vocal cords
tighten and your pitch rises. This is a biological stress response — the same
reason your voice cracks when you're anxious. Higher pitch signals submission
in virtually every primate species, including humans.

**How to lower your register:**
- **Morning warm-up:** Hum at the lowest comfortable pitch for 30 seconds
  each morning. This relaxes your vocal cords and sets your baseline.
- **Breathe from the diaphragm:** Shallow chest breathing produces a thin,
  higher voice. Deep belly breathing gives you resonance and power. Place
  your hand on your stomach — it should expand when you breathe in.
- **Speak from the chest, not the throat:** Imagine the sound originating
  from behind your sternum, not from your neck. This produces a richer,
  more resonant tone.
- **Before important moments:** Take three slow, deep breaths. This
  physically lowers your pitch by relaxing the muscles around your larynx.

**The target:** Not a fake baritone. A relaxed, resonant, grounded version
of your natural voice. Record yourself after 5 minutes of humming and
diaphragmatic breathing — you'll hear the difference.

### 1.2 Pace — Slow Down

**The principle:** Powerful people are never in a rush to speak. Speed
communicates anxiety. Deliberate pace communicates control.

**The numbers:**
- Anxious/low-status speech: 170-200+ words per minute
- Conversational speech: 150-170 words per minute
- Authoritative speech: 130-150 words per minute
- High-impact moments: 100-120 words per minute

**Why we rush:** You rush because you subconsciously fear the room will stop
listening. You feel you need to "get it all out" before someone interrupts or
loses interest. This is a low-status instinct. High-status speakers assume the
room will wait. And it does.

**How to slow down:**
- **The mental trick:** Pretend every sentence costs you $100. You'd choose
  your words more carefully and deliver them more slowly.
- **The physical trick:** Between sentences, take a full breath. Not a quick
  gasp — a real, complete inhale. This automatically creates pace.
- **Practice with a timer:** Read a 150-word paragraph in exactly 60 seconds.
  This is 150 wpm — it will feel painfully slow at first. That's the point.
  That "painful" pace is what authority sounds like.

**Example paragraph for pace practice (150 words):**
> "The proposal we're reviewing today represents a significant shift in our
> approach to market expansion. Over the past quarter, our team has analyzed
> three potential entry strategies, each with distinct risk profiles and
> resource requirements. The data consistently points toward a phased
> approach, beginning with the Southeast region where our existing
> relationships provide a natural advantage. This isn't a conservative play
> — it's a calculated one. The companies that succeed in new markets are
> rarely the first movers. They're the ones who enter with precision, learn
> fast, and scale deliberately. I'm recommending we commit to the phased
> approach with a six-month evaluation window. The investment is modest, the
> downside is contained, and the upside — if the Southeast market validates
> our thesis — positions us for national expansion in eighteen months."

Read that in exactly 60 seconds. Record yourself. Listen back.

### 1.3 The Power of Silence

**The rule:** After you make a key point, STOP TALKING.

Most people fill silence with noise because silence feels uncomfortable. But
silence after a statement is the verbal equivalent of a bold, underlined
headline. It says: "What I just said is important enough to sit with."

**Why silence works:**
- It gives the room time to process your point
- It demonstrates that YOU believe what you said is significant
- It signals confidence — you don't need to keep talking to justify yourself
- It creates contrast — the silence makes the next thing you say hit harder

**The three types of strategic silence:**

**The Landing Pause (2-3 seconds):** After stating a conclusion or
recommendation. Let it land.
> "We need to change direction on this." [2 seconds of silence] "Here's why."

**The Weight Pause (3-5 seconds):** After delivering a particularly
significant or surprising point. Let the room absorb it.
> "Our largest client is considering terminating the contract." [4 seconds]
> "I have a plan to prevent that."

**The Dominance Pause (2 seconds before speaking):** When asked a question,
don't answer immediately. Pause for 2 seconds, then respond. This signals
that you're thinking, not reacting. It projects composure.
> "Why did the project miss its deadline?"
> [2-second pause] "There were two primary factors. Let me walk through both."

**The discomfort is the point.** If the pause feels uncomfortably long to
you, it's the right length. Your internal clock overestimates silence when
you're the speaker.

### 1.4 Downward Inflection — The Sound of Certainty

**The mechanic:** Every declarative statement should end with your pitch
going DOWN. This is the single most important vocal technique for projecting
authority.

**Upward inflection (bad):**
> "We should move forward with this plan?" ↗

This asks for permission. It says: "Is that okay? Do you agree?"

**Downward inflection (good):**
> "We should move forward with this plan." ↘

This declares. It says: "This is what we're doing. I'm confident in this."

**How to practice:**
- Pick any sentence. Say it while moving your hand DOWNWARD on the last
  two words. Your voice will follow your hand.
- Record yourself reading five sentences. Listen for the pitch on the
  final word. If it rises, re-record until every one drops.
- Think of a period as a vocal cliff. Your pitch steps off the edge and
  drops.

**Practice sentences (say each with deliberate downward inflection):**
1. "The numbers support this direction."
2. "I've considered the alternatives."
3. "This is my recommendation."
4. "We don't have room for another delay."
5. "The decision is clear."
6. "I take full responsibility for this outcome."
7. "Here's what we're going to do."
8. "The current approach is not working."

## PART 2: THE BODY OF AUTHORITY

Your body broadcasts your internal state before you speak a single word.
A person with authoritative body language is perceived as a leader within
seconds of entering a room — before they've said anything.

### 2.1 Posture — Occupy Space

**The principle:** Confident people take up space. Anxious people shrink.
This is universal across cultures and even across species.

**The power posture:**
- **Shoulders:** Back and down. Not puffed up like a rooster — just
  naturally pulled back, opening your chest.
- **Chin:** Level with the ground. Not tilted up (arrogant) or down
  (submissive). Parallel to the floor.
- **Spine:** Imagine a string pulling the crown of your head toward the
  ceiling. Elongate.
- **Feet:** Shoulder-width apart when standing. Planted. No crossing, no
  shifting weight.
- **Arms:** At your sides or in controlled gestures. Never crossed (which
  signals defensiveness), and never in pockets (which signals discomfort).

**What NOT to do:**
- Cross your arms (defensive barrier)
- Touch your face, neck, or hair (self-soothing = anxiety)
- Lean against walls or furniture (lack of core confidence)
- Stand with feet together or crossed (unstable, submissive)
- Rock or sway (nervous energy with no outlet)
- Shrink into your chair (minimize yourself = minimize your authority)

**Seated power posture:**
- Sit back in the chair with your full back supported
- Both feet flat on the floor
- Hands on the table or armrests — visible
- Lean forward slightly when making a point (engagement)
- Lean back slightly when listening (composure, evaluation)

### 2.2 Eye Contact — Hold It

**The rule:** 3-5 seconds of direct eye contact per person before moving
to the next. Breaking eye contact downward signals submission. Breaking
sideways is neutral.

**Why eye contact matters:**
- Direct eye contact triggers trust responses in the brain
- People who maintain eye contact are rated as more confident, honest,
  and competent
- Avoiding eye contact (especially looking down) signals shame, fear,
  or submission

**How to build eye contact strength:**
- **The triangle technique:** If direct eye-to-eye contact feels too
  intense, look at the triangle formed by the other person's eyes and
  the bridge of their nose. They can't tell the difference.
- **In meetings:** When you speak, address one person per sentence. Hold
  eye contact with them for the full sentence, then move to the next
  person. Don't scan the room — it looks nervous.
- **When listening:** Maintain eye contact with the speaker about 70% of
  the time. This shows engagement without staring.
- **When being challenged:** Do NOT look away. Looking down when someone
  pushes back on your point is a submission signal. Hold eye contact,
  pause, then respond.

**The power gaze test:** In your next meeting, try maintaining eye contact
for a full 5 seconds with someone while making a point. Notice how it
changes the dynamic. Notice how they take your point more seriously.

### 2.3 Hand Gestures — Controlled and Open

**Power gestures:**
- **Open palms facing up:** "I'm being transparent, I have nothing to hide"
- **Open palms facing forward:** "This is definitive"
- **The steeple (fingertips touching):** Projects deep thought and certainty.
  This is the go-to gesture for executives during deliberation.
- **Counting on fingers:** When listing points ("First... Second... Third...")
  — signals structure and preparation
- **Hands at rest on the table:** Calm, grounded, ready

**Weak gestures to eliminate:**
- **Fidgeting** with pens, rings, hair, or clothing — screams anxiety
- **Wringing hands** — signals stress
- **Touching face or neck** — self-soothing, subconscious anxiety display
- **Hiding hands** under the table or in pockets — reduces trust
- **Excessive gesturing** — distracting, loses impact. Gesture with purpose.

**The stillness principle:** Between gestures, return to stillness. Let your
hands rest. The contrast between stillness and a deliberate gesture gives
the gesture power. Constant motion dilutes everything.

### 2.4 Entering a Room

**The rule:** Walk in like you belong there. Because you do.

**The power entrance:**
1. **Walk at a measured pace.** Not slow enough to look lost, not fast enough
   to look nervous. Deliberate.
2. **Head up, shoulders back.** You are not looking at your phone. You are
   not scanning the floor. Your chin is level and your eyes are forward.
3. **Walk to your seat with purpose.** Don't hover. Don't linger at the door.
   Don't look for permission. Walk to your seat, pull out your chair, sit
   down, and settle.
4. **Place your materials deliberately.** Notebook here. Pen here. Water
   there. This signals preparation and ownership of your space.
5. **Make eye contact with people as you sit down.** A nod, a brief
   acknowledgment. You're present.

**What NOT to do:**
- Rush in apologizing for being late (even if you are late — apologize once,
  briefly, then move on)
- Hover at the door waiting for someone to acknowledge you
- Sit down and immediately start scrolling your phone
- Shrink into the corner seat if you can avoid it

## PART 3: THE FIVE POWER TECHNIQUES

These are named, specific techniques to practice until they become
unconscious habits.

### Technique 1: The Strategic Pause

**What:** After making a key point, pause for 2-3 seconds. Let the room
absorb it. Do not fill the silence.

**When to use it:**
- After stating a recommendation or conclusion
- After sharing a significant data point
- After asking a question (let the silence compel an answer)
- Before answering a difficult question (shows composure)

**Practice script:**
> "The current strategy is not delivering results." [3 seconds]
> "I have a different approach to propose." [2 seconds]
> "It requires a significant investment." [2 seconds]
> "But the return profile makes it compelling."

Say this aloud. Hold EVERY pause. Resist the urge to fill it.

### Technique 2: The Downward Inflection

**What:** End every statement with falling pitch. "We're moving forward
with Option A." — the pitch drops on "A."

**The test:** Record yourself saying each of these. Play back. Is your
pitch dropping on the last word? If it rises even slightly, redo it.
1. "This is the direction we're taking."
2. "The data is unambiguous."
3. "I need a decision by Friday."
4. "We're not going to accept that."
5. "Let me be clear about this."

### Technique 3: The Steeple

**What:** Touch your fingertips together in front of your chest — forming
a "steeple" shape. This is the gesture of deliberation and certainty.

**When to use it:**
- While listening to someone else make a point (shows engaged evaluation)
- When pausing before answering a question (projects thoughtfulness)
- During a moment of disagreement (projects calm confidence)

**When NOT to use it:**
- While speaking (it can look disconnected from your words)
- Constantly (it loses its power — use it at key moments)

### Technique 4: Controlled Pace

**What:** Speak at 130-150 words per minute for authority. At high-impact
moments, slow to 100-120 wpm.

**How to calibrate:**
- Count the words in a paragraph
- Time your delivery
- Divide words by seconds, multiply by 60
- Adjust until you're in the 130-150 range

**The slow-down signal:** When you catch yourself speeding up, take a
breath between sentences. One real breath. This resets your pace.

### Technique 5: The Grounded Stance

**What:** When standing, feet shoulder-width apart, weight evenly
distributed across both feet. Do not shift, sway, or rock.

**Why:** A grounded stance communicates stability, confidence, and
control. It's the physical equivalent of saying "I'm not going anywhere."

**The test:** Stand and deliver a 30-second point. Have someone (or a
video camera) watch your feet. Are they planted? Or are you swaying,
shifting, rocking? If you're moving, anchor yourself and try again.

## PART 4: DAILY DRILLS

### Drill 1: The Authority Delivery (15 minutes)

Read each statement aloud with:
- Downward inflection on the final word
- A 2-3 second pause after each statement
- Zero filler words
- Controlled pace (130-150 wpm)
- Grounded stance (stand while delivering)

**Statements to deliver:**

1. "We need to reallocate resources to meet the Q4 target."

2. "I've reviewed the proposal. There are three areas that need revision."

3. "This is the direction we're taking. Let me walk you through the rationale."

4. "The data supports a different conclusion than what's been presented."

5. "I want to be direct. The current approach isn't working."

**Self-check after each statement:**
- [ ] Pitch dropped on the last word?
- [ ] Held silence for 2-3 seconds after?
- [ ] Zero filler words?
- [ ] Pace felt deliberate, not rushed?
- [ ] Body was still and grounded?

Record all five. Play back. Be brutally honest. Redo any that don't pass
every checkbox.

### Drill 2: The Pause Drill (10 minutes)

Read the first statement, then HOLD SILENCE for the indicated duration.
The silence should feel uncomfortable. That means you're doing it right.
Then deliver the follow-up.

**1.** "The numbers don't support this strategy."
→ **HOLD 3 SECONDS OF SILENCE**
→ "Here's what the data actually shows."

**2.** "I have a different perspective on this."
→ **HOLD 2 SECONDS OF SILENCE**
→ "Let me walk you through it."

**3.** "We have a decision to make."
→ **HOLD 3 SECONDS OF SILENCE**
→ "And I think the answer is clear."

**4.** "I want to be candid with you."
→ **HOLD 2 SECONDS OF SILENCE**
→ "This project is behind schedule."

**Additional pause exercises:**

**5.** "Our client retention rate dropped 15% this quarter."
→ **HOLD 4 SECONDS**
→ "That should concern everyone in this room."

**6.** "I've spent the last two weeks analyzing this."
→ **HOLD 2 SECONDS**
→ "And my conclusion is clear."

**7.** "There's something we need to discuss."
→ **HOLD 3 SECONDS**
→ "It's not going to be comfortable, but it's necessary."

**8.** "I disagree."
→ **HOLD 3 SECONDS**
→ "Here's why."

### Drill 3: The Mirror Drill (Daily — Non-Negotiable, 5 minutes)

This is your most important daily exercise for Week 2.

**Instructions:**
1. Stand in front of a mirror, feet shoulder-width apart
2. Make eye contact with your own reflection
3. Deliver a 60-second update on ANY topic (your project, the news, what
   you had for dinner — the topic doesn't matter)
4. While delivering, maintain:
   - Constant eye contact with your reflection
   - Zero filler words (carry over from Week 1)
   - Deliberate pauses after key points
   - Downward inflection on every statement
   - Still, grounded body — no fidgeting, swaying, or shifting
5. Record it on your phone (audio or video)
6. Play it back. Grade yourself.

**What you're training:** This drill ties together EVERYTHING from Weeks 1
and 2. Clean language + vocal authority + physical presence. It's the
integrated package.

### Drill 4: The Pace Calibration (10 minutes, twice this week)

Use the 150-word practice paragraph from Part 1.2.

1. Set a timer for 60 seconds
2. Begin reading the paragraph aloud
3. You should finish the paragraph right as the timer hits 60 seconds
4. If you finish early, you're rushing — slow down
5. If you don't finish, you've found the right pace (or even slower
   than needed, which is fine)
6. Record it. Listen. Notice how "slow" 150 wpm actually sounds.

### Drill 5: The Eye Contact Challenge (in real conversations)

This week, in at least 3 real conversations:
- Maintain eye contact for a full 5 seconds while making a point
- When someone challenges you, do NOT look away. Hold eye contact,
  pause, then respond
- In a meeting, deliver one sentence directly to one person with full
  eye contact before moving to the next person

Track how it changes the dynamic. Notice how people respond differently
when you hold their gaze.

## PART 5: THE INTEGRATED DELIVERY — PUTTING IT ALL TOGETHER

This is where Weeks 1 and 2 merge. The following scenarios require you to
combine clean language with vocal authority and physical presence.

### Scenario 1: The Project Update (stand and deliver)

Stand up. Feet planted. Shoulders back. Deliver this in under 45 seconds
with zero filler, downward inflection, and at least two strategic pauses:

> "The project is on track for March delivery. [PAUSE] We hit the API
> integration milestone last week, which was the highest-risk item on the
> timeline. Two items remain: the load testing phase, which starts Monday,
> and the security review, scheduled for the following week. [PAUSE] I don't
> anticipate any blockers, but I'm monitoring vendor response times closely.
> If they slip, I have a contingency plan ready."

### Scenario 2: The Difficult Point (seated, in a meeting)

Sit with power posture. Lean forward slightly. Make eye contact with an
imaginary colleague across the table. Deliver this:

> "I want to be direct about something. [PAUSE] The timeline we agreed
> to in January is no longer realistic. The scope has expanded twice since
> then, and we haven't adjusted the deadline. [PAUSE] I'm recommending we
> extend by three weeks. The alternative is shipping something we're not
> confident in, and I don't think that serves anyone."

### Scenario 3: The Confident Disagreement (standing, to a group)

Stand. Grounded stance. Steeple gesture while listening to the
"opposing view." Then drop hands to sides, make eye contact, and deliver:

> "I see the logic in that approach. [PAUSE] And I've arrived at a
> different conclusion. [PAUSE] The data from Q3 shows that the strategy
> you're proposing was tested in the Southeast market and underperformed
> by 22%. My recommendation is we take the lessons from that pilot and
> design a different entry strategy. Let me walk you through what I
> have in mind."

## PART 6: COMMON MISTAKES AND CORRECTIONS

| Mistake | Why It Happens | The Fix |
|---------|---------------|---------|
| Voice rises when challenged | Stress response tightens vocal cords | Take a breath before responding. Consciously push pitch down. |
| Rushing through points | Fear of losing the room's attention | Trust the room. One point at a time. Breathe between sentences. |
| Filling every silence | Discomfort with quiet | Practice silence tolerance. 3 seconds of silence is 3 seconds, not an eternity. |
| Looking down when disagreed with | Submission instinct | Pre-commit: before meetings, decide you will NOT break eye contact downward. |
| Fidgeting with pen/phone | Nervous energy outlet | Put the pen down. Phone face-down or away. Hands on table, at rest. |
| Swaying when standing | Ungrounded stance habit | Plant feet shoulder-width, lock in. Imagine roots going into the floor. |
| Speaking from the throat | Shallow breathing | Start each speaking occasion with 3 diaphragmatic breaths. |
| Over-gesturing | Trying to add energy to weak delivery | Let the WORDS carry the weight. Gesture only to emphasize a specific point. |

## PART 9: PRINCIPLES TO INTERNALIZE

1. **Your voice is the first thing the room judges.** Before they process
   your words, they've already decided if you sound authoritative. Tune
   the instrument.

2. **Silence is a weapon.** The pause after a statement isn't dead air —
   it's where your point gains weight. Use it aggressively.

3. **Stillness is power.** Constant movement bleeds energy and attention
   away from your message. Be still. Move with purpose. Return to still.

4. **Your body talks louder than your mouth.** If your words say "I'm
   confident" but your body says "I'm nervous," the room believes
   your body.

5. **Slow is smooth, smooth is fast.** When you slow down, you sound more
   articulate, more prepared, and more in control. You actually communicate
   MORE by speaking LESS quickly.

6. **Eye contact is a commitment.** Looking someone in the eye while you
   make a point says: "I believe this, and I'm telling YOU directly."
   Looking away says: "I'm not sure about this, and I'd rather not be
   challenged."

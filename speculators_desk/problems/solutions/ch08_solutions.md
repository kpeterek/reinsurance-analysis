### Solution 8.1
(a) **Nowhere in recurring NOI.** A termination fee is capital paid in exchange for forgone future rent. Record it as a one-time item, and underwrite the space as vacant. If the fee is large relative to the remaining rent, it partly offsets the re-leasing cost, which affects the price but not the NOI.
(b) **Nowhere.** Reverse it to get cash rent. A positive adjustment means GAAP revenue exceeds cash, usually because of recent free rent.
(c) **Other income (EGI).** It is recurring, but month-to-month permits make it less durable than leased rent. Underwrite it at a haircut or test it against nearby parking rates and the building's occupancy.
(d) **Below NOI (capital).** It is covered by reserves on a stabilized basis. If it is due now, deduct it from price (Example 8.5).
(e) **Nowhere at the property level.** A fund fee is an owner-level cost. Deduct it in the investor's return, not in NOI.
(f) **Below NOI (TI/LC in NCF).** It is a cost of acquiring rent.
(g) **Operating expense, or a separate senior line above NCF.** Ground rent is a claim senior to the owner's. Either treatment works if stated. What does not work is ignoring it, or capitalizing NOI before ground rent at a fee-simple cap rate. *Depends on:* the ground rent's reset terms, which can make it far more burdensome than its current amount.
(h) **Nowhere.** Non-cash.
(i) **Nowhere in recurring NOI.** A refund relates to a prior year. It may also signal that the current appraisal is protestable, which is information about future tax, not income.
(j) **Operating expense** (below GOP, in the fees section of the USALI statement; Chapter 11). It is a recurring cost of the brand.

### Solution 8.4
(a) Occupied area 90,000 sf: physical occupancy 90.0%. Base rent $= 300{,}000 + 200{,}000 + 135{,}000 + 85{,}000 = \$720{,}000$.
(b) $WALT_R = (1{,}500{,}000 + 300{,}000 + 405{,}000 + 63{,}750)/720{,}000 = 2{,}268{,}750/720{,}000 = 3.15$ years. $WALT_A = (200{,}000 + 37{,}500 + 45{,}000 + 7{,}500)/90{,}000 = 3.22$ years.
(c) T2 and T4 roll within 24 months: $(200{,}000 + 85{,}000)/720{,}000 = 39.6\%$.
(d) Market rent on occupied space $= 330{,}000 + 218{,}750 + 135{,}000 + 92{,}500 = \$776{,}250$. Mark-to-market $= +\$56{,}250$ (+7.81%). Realizable within 24 months: T2 $25{,}000 \times 0.75 = 18{,}750$ plus T4 $10{,}000 \times 0.75 = 7{,}500$, a total of \$26,250.
(e) PGI $= 720{,}000 + 10{,}000 \times 9.00 + 250{,}000 = \$1{,}060{,}000$.
(f) Recoveries $= 2.50 \times 90{,}000 = \$225{,}000$ (the landlord absorbs \$25,000 for the vacant suite). EGI $= 720{,}000 + 225{,}000 = \$945{,}000$. OpEx $= 250{,}000 + 0.03 \times 945{,}000 + 0.10 \times 100{,}000 = 250{,}000 + 28{,}350 + 10{,}000 = \$288{,}350$. NOI $= \$656{,}650$. NCF $= 656{,}650 - 15{,}000 - 50{,}000 = \$591{,}650$.

Interpretation: 40% of rent rolls within two years, but the near-term rollover is *below* market, so renewals or re-leasing should raise rent, by \$26,250 a year if the space re-leases at market. Against that, each rollover costs TI/LC and possibly downtime. The \$0.50/sf TI/LC reserve (\$50,000 a year) is roughly two years of the realizable mark-to-market. Underwrite the T2 renewal probability explicitly.

### Solution 8.5
Face rents: $28.00, 28.84, 29.71, 30.60, 31.51, 32.46, 33.43, 34.44, 35.47, 36.53$.
(a) Aggregate face rent $= 28 \times \frac{1.03^{10} - 1}{0.03} = \$320.99$/sf.
(b) Year-1 cash rent $= 28 \times 2/12 = \$4.67$; aggregate cash rent $= 320.99 - 28 + 4.67 = \$297.66$/sf.
(c) LC $= 5\% \times 297.66 = \$14.88$/sf.
(d) PV of cash rent at 8% $= \$189.80$/sf.
(e) $a_{\overline{10}|8\%} = 6.71008$. NER $= (189.80 - 55 - 14.88)/6.71008 = \$17.87$/sf, which is 63.8% of the \$28.00 starting rent. (Undiscounted NER $= (297.66 - 55 - 14.88)/10 = \$22.78$.)
(f) Straight-line rent $= 297.66/10 = \$29.77$/sf per year.
(g) The face rate is a starting point in a schedule of escalating rents. The landlord gives back 10 months of rent, \$55/sf of TI, and \$14.88/sf of commissions, all up front, and receives rent over ten years. In present value the landlord earns the equivalent of \$17.87 a year, level. GAAP reports \$29.77 a year, which is *higher* than face because escalations are averaged in. Three different numbers describe one lease: face \$28.00, GAAP \$29.77, economic \$17.87. Comparable rents for valuation should be expressed as NER.

### Solution 8.6
(a) Base: RevPAR \$85.00; total revenue \$3,946,380; GOP \$1,751,409; NOI \$802,862 (NOI margin 20.3%).
(b) Both cases have RevPAR \$72.25 and total revenue \$3,354,423.

(c) Values at 9.5%: base \$8,451,181; occupancy-led \$5,209,820; ADR-led \$3,704,944. Loan $= 0.60 \times 8{,}451{,}181 = \$5{,}070{,}709$. Annual debt service at 7.5%, 25 years, monthly $= \$449{,}665$. DSCR: base 1.79x; occupancy-led 1.10x; ADR-led 0.78x.
(d) A downside written as "RevPAR −15%" is incomplete. The buyer must say whether the decline comes through rate or volume, because the ADR-led case cuts NOI by half again as much and turns DSCR below 1.0x. In a downturn caused by new supply, competitors often defend occupancy by cutting rate, which is the worse case. Write the downside as rate-led unless there is evidence otherwise, and widen the cap rate as well (Chapter 11).

### Solution 8.7
(a) Reserve $= 0.25 \times 80{,}000 = \$20{,}000$. NCF $= \$880{,}000$. Stabilized value $= 880{,}000/0.0725 = \$12{,}137{,}931$.
(b) Roof PV $= 600{,}000/1.08^3 = \$476{,}299$. Excess HVAC $= (40{,}000 - 20{,}000) \times a_{\overline{5}|8\%} = 20{,}000 \times 3.99271 = \$79{,}854$.
(c) Price $= 12{,}137{,}931 - 350{,}000 - 476{,}299 - 79{,}854 = \$11{,}231{,}777$. The ask, $900{,}000/0.0725 = \$12{,}413{,}793$, is \$1,182,016 (9.5%) higher.
(d) *Counter-proposal.* Price \$11.23 million, or \$11.58 million with the seller's \$350,000 credit (the same economics). Explain that the reserve is permanent and therefore capitalized ($20{,}000/0.0725 = \$275{,}862$), the roof is a known liability whose PV is \$476,299 whether it is paid in year 3 or today, and the HVAC excess is \$79,854. If the seller resists on the roof, offer an escrow funded at closing and released when the roof is replaced, which converts the disagreement about price into a disagreement about cost. *Lease clauses:* CAM definitions (whether capital replacements may be amortized and billed), CAM caps, exclusions for anchors, and the remaining terms of the tenants who would pay. If, say, half of the parking-lot cost is recoverable over its useful life from tenants with long remaining terms, the price deduction falls by the PV of those recoveries. Recoveries from tenants with short terms are worth little, because the tenants may leave before paying.

*Rubric (Level 4).* Full credit: correct separation of permanent and one-time items; a counter that accepts the parking-lot credit but deducts the roof PV and excess HVAC; an escrow alternative; identification of CAM capital-recovery clauses and caps; recognition that recoverability is worth only what remaining lease terms can pay. Partial: correct price but no negotiation structure. None: capitalizing the roof at the cap rate, or accepting "the roof is three years away."

### Solution 8.9
(a) Texas: $V = 2{,}000{,}000/(0.055 + 0.024) = \$25{,}316{,}456$ (tax \$607,595). California: $V = 2{,}000{,}000/(0.055 + 0.0122) = \$29{,}761{,}905$ (tax \$363,095).
(b) $(2{,}000{,}000 - 60{,}000)/0.055 = \$35{,}272{,}727$, an overpayment of \$5,510,823 (18.5%).
(c) California year-5 tax $= 363{,}095 \times 1.02^4 = \$393{,}026$. Texas year-5 tax $= 607{,}595 \times 1.03^4 = \$683{,}853$.
(d) Lower ongoing tax is already reflected in the loaded value: holding $c$ equal, the California building is worth 17.6% more per dollar of pre-tax NOI. Lowering $c$ as well counts the tax advantage twice. The legitimate additional effect is growth: under Proposition 13, the tax line grows at most 2% a year while the owner holds, so after-tax NOI grows slightly faster than in a market-value regime. That justifies at most a small reduction in $c$, and only if market values are expected to rise faster than 2%. The benefit disappears at resale, because the next buyer's base resets, so it must not be capitalized into the exit value.

*Rubric (Level 4).* Full credit: correct loaded values; identifies the double count; distinguishes the level effect (in the loaded value) from the growth effect (a small cap-rate or DCF adjustment); notes the reset at resale. Partial: correct numbers without the double-count argument.

### Solution 8.10 — Rubric (Level 5; abstention is the best default)
(a) Reassessed tax at \$30.0 million $= 0.023 \times 30{,}000{,}000 = \$690{,}000$, versus the seller's \$414,000 ($0.023 \times 18{,}000{,}000$). NOI after reassessment $= 2{,}400{,}000 - 276{,}000 = \$2{,}124{,}000$. Yield on price 7.08%.

(c) Ranked information: (1) the 60,000 sf tenant's renewal intentions, and whether it is dark or sub-leasing; (2) the TI and LC obligations outstanding and those likely on the 41% rollover; (3) the operating-expense detail and whether the \$2.4 million NOI is cash or straight-line; (4) leasing comparables expressed as NER; (5) the submarket's vacancy and sublease availability.
(d)

| Dimension | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| Arithmetic | Reassesses tax; deducts free rent; recognizes that the asking cap is on the seller's tax bill |
| Rollover | Treats 41% expiring in 18 months as the dominant risk; notes that one tenant is 24% of the building, so the outcome is close to binary |

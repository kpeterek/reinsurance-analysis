### Solution 12.1
(a) **Liquidity problem; no demand loss.** Value is 1.4 times the loan, so equity is about 29% of value. The refinance gap of \$6 million is a financing shock from the rate reset. The owner can sell, bring in preferred equity, or fund a capital call, and partners should fund it because the equity is worth far more than the gap. *Would change:* evidence that the 95% occupancy depends on one tenant with an imminent expiry.

(b) **Structural demand loss, at least for this building**; liquidity or solvency cannot be judged without the debt. Tenants shrinking at renewal with unchanged headcount is the signature of lower use per worker, not of a recession. *Would change:* evidence that the tenants moved the remaining space to another building nearby (a relocation, not a reduction in use), or that headcount is about to grow.

(c) **Neither yet, and the demand loss looks cyclical.** Eighteen months of reserves and three years to maturity give the owner time. *Would change:* a recession that lasts longer than the reserve, a franchise PIP due before maturity, or evidence of new supply in the competitive set (which would make part of the loss persistent).

(d) **Solvency problem in the making; supply shock (cyclical).** Value is about equal to the loan, so the equity is worth roughly nothing after selling costs. The cap expiry raises debt service in four months. Fourteen percent of stock delivered in two years is a supply shock that should be absorbed over time if demand keeps growing. *Would change:* the size of the remaining pipeline (Example 12.1), the sponsor's willingness to buy a new cap, and whether the lender will extend.

### Solution 12.8

| Price | Denominator | Par sold | After-tax loss | Capital after |

At 70 the bank sells half its CRE book; at 65 it sells 84% and is left with \$22.7 million of capital. Selling at those prices is self-defeating.
(b) $420/3 - 120 = \$20$ million of new capital.
(c) **Rubric.**

| Dimension | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| Arithmetic | Shows the feedback denominator and the three sale amounts |
| Recommendation | Sells only the loans that clear near par (85+), raises some capital or retains earnings for the rest, and works out deep-discount loans rather than selling them |
| Supervisory framing | Notes that the screen is not a limit: the board's two-quarter target is a choice, and the real constraint is capital adequacy and examiner confidence |
| Buyer's inference | The bank will offer loans that trade near par first. Loans that would clear at 60–70 are unlikely to be offered until capital is raised or losses are absorbed. A buyer should expect adverse selection toward good loans in early sales and should price later pools for what the bank chose to keep |
| Abstention | Recognizes that a buyer seeking 65-cent loans from this bank may have nothing to buy for several quarters |

### Solution 12.9
Occupied area $= 250{,}000 \times 0.85 = 212{,}500$ sf. Fixed costs $= 2.75 + 0.875 = \$3.625$ million.
(a) You at 6.5%: $(0.065 \times 95{,}000{,}000 + 3{,}625{,}000)/212{,}500 = \$46.12$. New owner at 10%: $(4{,}000{,}000 + 3{,}625{,}000)/212{,}500 = \$35.88$. Debt-service break-even: $(4{,}200{,}000 + 3{,}625{,}000)/212{,}500 = \$36.82$.
(b) At \$40.00: NOI $= 8{,}500{,}000 - 3{,}625{,}000 = \$4{,}875{,}000$; DSCR $= 1.16x$; yield on basis 5.13%. At \$36.00: NOI $= \$4{,}025{,}000$; DSCR $= 0.96x$; yield 4.24%.
(c) **Rubric (no single correct answer).**

| Dimension | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| Diagnosis | You cannot earn 6.5% at market rent; that return is gone. The new owner's \$35.88 is below your debt-service break-even (\$36.82), so a rent war ends with you below 1.0x |
| Options | *Cut rent*: preserves occupancy, but at \$36 you cannot pay debt service. *Hold rent*: loses tenants at renewal to the new owner. *Modify*: approach your lender now, while you are current, with the basis-reset arithmetic; a lender comparing your 0.96x to a foreclosure at a \$40 million clearing basis may accept an extension with paydown or an A/B note. *Sell*: a sale near \$40–45 million wipes out your \$25 million of equity and leaves a shortfall to the lender |
| Equity view | Your equity is now an option on recovery. Funding a capital call to defend it is rational only if recovery is likely before maturity and the lender will not restructure |
| Lender view | The lender's alternative is a foreclosure that creates another low-basis competitor. That is your negotiating leverage |
| Abstention | "Do nothing and hope" is not abstention; it is a decision to let the lender choose the timing |

### Solution 12.10 — Rubric (Level 5)

Score each dimension 0–4. A strong answer scores at least 3 on every dimension.

| Dimension | Strong answer includes |
|---|---|
| Value range | Refuses to use the stale \$58 million appraisal. Rebuilds value from ~50% occupancy, with a scenario in which the 30% tenant leaves (occupancy near 20%). States leasing costs and capex. Acknowledges a range that may run from well below half of the old appraisal to the old figure, and that the information cannot narrow it in five days |
| Right to match | Explains that a right of first refusal held by an insider creates a winner's curse: you win only when the insider, who knows more, declines to match, which is when your price is too high. Expected return conditional on winning is lower than your unconditional estimate |
| Litigation and timeline | The sponsor's lawsuit and a judicial process stretch resolution; models multi-year timing, value drift, and legal costs (Example 12.4 logic). Notes that the guaranty's absence leaves the borrower's incentive to settle unknown |
| Information needed | Current rent roll and leases, the tenant's notice, a broker opinion or new appraisal, the guaranty, the PSA terms on the right to match, the litigation filings |
| Decision | **Abstention or a request for time is the best answer** unless the price is low enough to absorb the worst case and the winner's curse. A bid "to see what happens" is not acceptable: if the insider declines to match, you own the asset at the price you named |

Answers that bid off the old appraisal, ignore the right to match, or treat judicial timing as months score 0–1 on the relevant dimension.

### Decision exercise (§12.10) — Rubric and model answer

The rubric table is in §12.10. A model answer:

* **A (NPL).** Bid \$23.5–24.0 million (about 59–60% of UPB) only after a borrower and guarantor review. Withdraw if the probability of contested or bankruptcy resolution rises above roughly 50% combined, which takes the maximum bid at 16% below \$23.4 million. Commission an independent valuation; the broker's \$31 million drives every title scenario.
* **B (office tower).** Case E: at the \$52 million ask the IRR of expected flows is about 4%; a 12% unlevered target supports about \$29 million. Bid near that level with a walk-away above \$32 million, or pass. Replacement cost is irrelevant because the market rent (\$34) is about half the rent new construction needs (about \$64).
* **C (REIT).** The 24% discount is a 99 bp cap-rate disagreement. Hold a small position (a few percent of the allocation) without a catalyst. Increase only on evidence of private trades near 5.5% or on a credible asset-sale program.
* **Correlation.** All three are exposed to the same metro's office demand. Combined exposure should be sized so that a structural-demand outcome (Case E's Structural scenario) does not impair more than a stated fraction of the allocation.
* **Re-entry triggers.** A bank capital raise (more loans offered at lower prices), a CMBS control shift in a loan you follow, or REIT asset sales at stated cap rates.
